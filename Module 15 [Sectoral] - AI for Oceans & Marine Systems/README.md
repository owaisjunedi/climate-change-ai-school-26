### Key takeaways from today's lecture:





1. The ocean is central to the Earth's climate. The ocean is a fundamental component of the Earth's climate system, it covers about 70% of Earth’s surface and contains 97% of its water while absorbing about 90% of the excess heat, roughly 25% of anthropogenic CO2, and producing more than half of the world's oxygen. Its central role in regulating heat, carbon, and oxygen makes it fundamental to understanding and predicting climate change.



2. Ocean circulation emerges from simple physical principles. Global ocean circulation is driven by differences in temperature and salinity that create density contrasts, causing water to sink and rise in different regions. Although these processes are governed by relatively simple physics, they produce complex circulation patterns that strongly influence the Earth's climate.



3. Climate change affects many interconnected ocean processes. Climate change is warming the oceans, raising sea levels, increasing acidification, and intensifying marine heatwaves. These physical changes cascade through marine ecosystems, affecting plankton, fisheries, and the services the ocean provides.



4. Plankton are much more important than many people realize. Plankton form the foundation of the marine food web and play a crucial role in primary production and carbon sequestration. Changes in plankton communities therefore have significant consequences for marine biodiversity and global fisheries.



5. No single source of ocean data is sufficient. Ocean scientists rely on in-situ observations, satellite remote sensing, and numerical models, each of which provides only part of the overall picture. Integrating these complementary data sources is essential for accurately studying and predicting ocean processes.



6. Machine learning bridges physics and data. Machine learning fills the gap between simplified theoretical descriptions and realistic but difficult-to-interpret numerical models, enabling researchers to learn complex relationships directly from data. It is particularly valuable when explicit physical equations are unavailable, such as estimating ocean surface pCO₂ from satellite observations, demonstrating that ML is the bridge, not the destination.
