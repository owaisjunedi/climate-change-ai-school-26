This module, titled **"Module 9: AI in Transportation"** (presented by Cathy Wu from MIT for the Climate Change AI Virtual Summer School 2026), explores how artificial intelligence can be leveraged to mitigate climate change across the transportation sector.

The module provides a comprehensive framework to evaluate **where AI offers genuine high-leverage solutions, where its impacts are limited, and where technological interventions risk causing rebound effects or equity harms**.

---

### **1. Why Transportation is Hard to Decarbonize**
The module outlines several systemic sources of inertia in the transportation sector:
* **Growing Demand:** Travel and freight demand continually expand with rising incomes, urbanization, and e-commerce growth.
* **Slow Capital Stock Turnover:** Transportation assets (vehicles, charging stations, power grids, roads) turn over on long 15–20 year cycles.
* **Fragmented Decision-Making:** Passenger transport depends on billions of individual household decisions based on convenience and cost, whereas freight depends on firms, fleet operators, and logistics networks.
* **Rebound Effects (Jevons Paradox):** Efficiency gains can backfire by lowering travel costs, which increases total Vehicle Miles Traveled (VMT).

---

### **2. Core Mitigation Frameworks & AI Applications**

The material organizes AI interventions around three core pillars:

#### **A. AI for Electrification (Improve)**
* **Battery & Power Management:** Reinforcement learning (RL) models optimize real-time battery energy allocation to extend driving ranges and lifespans.
* **Grid & Charger Siting:** AI-driven simulations analyze charging behavior and grid loads to optimize electric vehicle (EV) charging infrastructure and vehicle-to-grid (V2G) dispatching.
* **Lifecycle Caveats:** The module stresses that EV emission savings depend heavily on powering vehicles with clean energy and mitigating the embodied carbon and critical mineral demands of battery production.

#### **B. AI for Modal Shift (Shift)**
* **Public Transit Optimization:** Predictive demand modeling helps transit agencies schedule routes, improve arrival-time reliability, and integrate first/last-mile options.
* **Multi-Modal Mobility:** Integrating walking, cycling, micromobility, rail, and public transit to make low-carbon travel options faster and more convenient than private cars.
* **Behavioral Simulation:** Utilizing Large Language Models (LLMs) and agent-based models to simulate human travel choices and refine transportation policies.

#### **C. AI for Transport Efficiency & Logistics (Improve & Avoid)**
* **Eco-Driving & Traffic Signals:** AI traffic signal control and green-light speed advisories smooth traffic flow at intersections, where a significant portion of urban vehicle emissions is wasted idling.
* **Freight Routing & Consolidation:** Machine learning accelerates route optimization, load matching, and freight consolidation (e.g., UPS's ORION system), drastically reducing empty backhaul miles.
* **Travel Avoidance (Immobility):** Telepresence and AI-optimized delivery platforms batch trips to reduce the overall need for physical travel.

---

### **3. Autonomous Vehicles (AVs) & Rebound Risks**
The module addresses the dual nature of **Autonomous Vehicles**:
* **The Promise:** AVs can enable eco-driving, platooning, smoother traffic, and shared electric transit fleets.
* **The Risk:** Making driving easier and cheaper risks increasing overall driving volume and displacing public transit, walking, or cycling.
* **The Verdict:** AVs are not inherently climate-positive unless they are **electric, shared, transit-complementary, and properly governed**.

---

### **4. Policy Alignment & Just Transition**
Decarbonizing transportation lacks a single economic champion; therefore, success requires aligning climate goals with diverse stakeholder priorities—such as national energy security, middle-class commuting costs, and corporate operating efficiency. Additionally, solutions must support a **swift, just, and equitable transition** that protects vulnerable populations and avoids excluding low-income communities.

### Key takeaways from today's lecture:

- Transportation is uniquely hard to decarbonize. It's the largest source of US GHG emissions, and unlike other sectors it lacks a single central actor pushing for change. Demand keeps growing with income and urbanization, assets (vehicles, roads, grids) turn over on decade-long cycles, and efficiency gains often rebound into more travel rather than less (the Jevons paradox).

- The Avoid-Shift-Improve (ASI) framework organizes where AI can help. Avoid reduces the need to travel (telepresence, delivery consolidation), Shift moves trips to lower-emission modes (transit, biking, multi-modal planning), and Improve reduces emissions per trip (electrification, efficient vehicles, traffic operations). AI shows up differently in each.

- AI's clearest wins are in electrification and freight. Reinforcement learning is already improving EV battery management and bidirectional (vehicle-to-grid) charging, in some cases cutting costs significantly over standard baselines. AI-driven freight routing and consolidation is another high-leverage use case, since it directly cuts empty miles and idle time.

- Autonomous vehicles are not inherently climate-positive. They could support eco-driving, platooning, and shared electric fleets, but they also risk increasing total vehicle travel and displacing walking, cycling, and transit if not paired with sharing and strong governance. The climate benefit depends entirely on how they're deployed.

- It's still early days, and AI's value depends on matching the right tool to the right bottleneck. AI is useful for building data-driven models, optimizing decisions, and automating workflows, but real progress requires figuring out which of these is actually the limiting factor in a given system, and building a broad coalition of stakeholders whose priorities happen to align with climate goals.
