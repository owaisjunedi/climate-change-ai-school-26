This first module, **"Tackling Climate Change with Machine Learning,"** presented by Maria João Sousa, David Rolnick, and Priya L. Donti, lays out the core foundation for how artificial intelligence can—and should—be used for climate action. 

---

### 1. Climate Action Framing: Mitigation vs. Adaptation
Before applying technology, we have to understand the two main ways we address climate change:
*   **Mitigation (Stopping the Cause):** Actions that reduce or prevent greenhouse gas emissions to slow down climate change. 
    *   *Analogy:* Turning off the tap when a sink is overflowing.
*   **Adaptation (Dealing with the Effects):** Actions that help communities and ecosystems withstand or recover from climate impacts that are already happening. 
    *   *Analogy:* Mopping up the floor and putting out sandbags to protect the house from water damage.
    *   **Robustness & Resilience:** Adaptation focuses on making systems **robust** (able to withstand impacts with minimal damage) and **resilient** (able to recover quickly if damage occurs).
*   **Climate Justice & Equity:** Effective climate solutions must put fairness, human rights, and social equity at the center, ensuring solutions benefit local communities (co-benefits linked to Sustainable Development Goals) rather than creating new inequalities.

### 2. AI vs. Machine Learning (The Basics)
The module clarifies what we actually mean by "AI":
*   **Artificial Intelligence (AI):** Any computer algorithm designed to perform complex tasks that typically require human intelligence.
*   **Rule-Based Systems:** Traditional software where humans explicitly program every single rule ("if X happens, do Y").
*   **Machine Learning (ML):** A specific subset of AI where the system automatically discovers patterns and learns from data rather than following hand-coded rules.
*   **How ML is Categorized:** Machine learning tools are usually classified by **data type** (images, text, time series), **problem type** (classification, prediction, optimization), and **model structure** (neural networks/deep learning, decision trees).

---

### 3. The 7 Technological Roles of AI in Climate Action
AI is not a single magic wand; instead, it serves as a versatile toolkit with 7 main roles:
1.  **Gathering Information:** Automatically analyzing satellite imagery to track deforestation, flood damage, or crop health.
2.  **Forecasting:** Predicting renewable energy production (sun/wind) or extreme weather events.
3.  **Optimizing Complex Systems:** Fine-tuning heating and cooling in big buildings or routing power through the electric grid efficiently.
4.  **Predictive Maintenance:** Detecting gas line methane leaks or infrastructure weakness before failure happens.
5.  **Accelerating Experimentation:** Speeding up the discovery of new materials for better batteries or carbon capture.
6.  **Approximating Simulations:** Replacing slow, computationally heavy physics simulations with fast ML approximations.
7.  **Data Management:** Combining scattered, messy data across different sources into usable formats.

---

### 4. Pathways to Real-World Impact & Responsible AI
*   **Avoid "Techno-Solutionism":** AI is not a silver bullet and cannot fix climate change on its own. It must be combined with policy, engineering, and community action.
*   **Consider AI's Own Carbon Footprint:** Running massive AI models consumes significant electricity in data centers, so we must weigh the energy costs of AI against its climate benefits.
*   **Bottom-Up Needs:** Standard "big data" AI models often fail in real-world climate problems because physical systems have strict safety limits, physical laws, and data constraints. Real-world climate AI requires specialized approaches like **physics-informed ML**, **safe/robust control**, and **energy-efficient ML**.
