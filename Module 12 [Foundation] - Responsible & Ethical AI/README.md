This module, titled **"AI: Responsibility in a Changing World"** (presented by Prof. Dr. Virginia Dignum, Chair of Responsible AI and Director of the AI Policy Lab at Umeå University), focuses on **Responsible AI, AI Ethics, Governance, and Climate Justice**.

It moves beyond viewing AI as a purely technical optimization tool and examines the broader **socio-technical ecosystem, ethical trade-offs, environmental costs, and governance frameworks** required when applying AI to societal and climate challenges.

---

### **1. Critiquing the "AI-First" Hype & "Question Zero"**
* **Moving Beyond Unchecked Deployment:** The module critiques the hype around "AI-First" strategies, which often push organizations to adopt AI without evaluating whether it delivers genuine productivity gains or addresses real societal needs.
* **Asking "Question Zero":** Rather than asking *how* to build an AI system, developers and policymakers must first ask **"Why AI?"** and **"Is AI the best or necessary option here?"**.
* **Challenging "More is Better":** The presentation challenges the assumption that scaling up compute, algorithms, and data is inherently positive, pointing to the massive energy consumption, carbon footprint, and water usage required by modern data centers.

---

### **2. Core Ethical Pillars: Datification, Power, and Sustainability**
* **Datification & Bias:** Data is not a direct reflection of reality; it is constructed, context-dependent, and inherently biased. 
* **Power Dynamics & Inequality:** The current AI ecosystem suffers from a concentration of power—spanning who decides, who develops the technology, who benefits, and who carries the physical and labor costs (e.g., low-wage data annotation workers).
* **Redefining Key Concepts:**
  * **Privacy:** Defined through *contextual integrity* rather than simple regulatory compliance.
  * **Accountability:** Defined as *answerability plus attributability*—ensuring there is an identifiable entity that can be held responsible.
  * **Robustness & Validity:** Ensuring models maintain performance under real-world distribution shifts.

---

### **3. Responsible AI in Climate Change Applications**
The module specifically illustrates how unethical or naive AI design leads to real-world failures in climate action:
* **Problem Scoping:** What is framed as the problem shapes the entire solution (e.g., "predicting agricultural yield" vs. "supporting a smallholder farmer's decision").
* **Flawed Target Variables & Proxies:** Relying on poor proxies creates severe distortions. For example, a flood risk model trained on *insurance damage claims* misclassifies uninsured households and informal settlements as "unflooded," systematically ignoring vulnerable communities.
* **Data Coverage Gaps:** Models validated only where data is abundant (e.g., urban weather stations) often fail in rural or developing regions where climate exposure is highest.
* **Three Levels of Climate Impact:** Evaluates AI's footprint across **Compute** (direct energy/water costs), **Application** (immediate deployment effects), and **System** (long-term structural changes in society).
* **Climate Justice:** Demands three forms of justice:
  1. *Distributive Justice:* Who gets the benefits vs. who bears the land, water, compute, and labor costs?
  2. *Procedural Justice:* Who was in the room during design decisions?
  3. *Recognitional Justice:* Whose knowledge and evidence are valued?

---

### **4. Operationalizing Governance & Multidisciplinary Innovation**
* **Ethics as a Process, Not a Technical Patch:** Ethics is an ongoing social process of recognizing trade-offs (e.g., accuracy vs. explainability, equity vs. equality, safety vs. privacy), not a feature to be programmed or a box-checking exercise.
* **Governance Frameworks:** Outlines international, regional, and national governance instruments—including the **EU AI Act**, **OECD Principles**, **UNESCO Recommendations**, **IEEE Ethically Aligned Design**, and the **UN AI Advisory Body**.
* **Design for Values:** Legal and ethical considerations must be integrated into the system from inception through value-sensitive design, documentation, and external oversight.

### Key takeaways from today's lecture:

1. “AI first" is the wrong starting point. Rushing to deploy AI without asking "why AI?" or "whether AI?" creates a democratic deficit, ignores mixed evidence on productivity gains, and pressures adoption without real evaluation.

2. AI is not neutral. It embodies human choices about data, power, and values, so datification (data is constructed and biased, not raw truth), concentration of power in who builds and decides, and the computational/human costs of AI all deserve scrutiny before deployment.

3. Ask "Question Zero" before anything else. The real question isn't how to build an AI system well, but whether it should be built at all, whether the problem is even correctly framed, and who bears the cost if the answer is wrong. This applies across the full lifecycle: framing, data, modeling, evaluation, deployment, and eventual decommissioning.

4. Key concepts are contested, not settled metrics. Bias, fairness, transparency, privacy, and accountability are normative and social, not purely technical terms. Choosing a fairness criterion, for instance, is itself a value judgment, not a neutral calculation.

5. Responsible AI is a social process, not a technical fix. Good implementation doesn't "solve" ethics. It requires ongoing governance (regulation, standards, participation), multidisciplinary collaboration, and human judgment, especially since as Victoria puts it, the smarter AI gets, the more we need human wisdom.
