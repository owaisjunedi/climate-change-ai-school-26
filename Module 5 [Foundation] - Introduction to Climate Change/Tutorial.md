This tutorial guide—**"Simulating Climate Futures in En-ROADS"**—was created by **Climate Interactive** in collaboration with **MIT Sloan** (and reviewed by researchers from MIT and Climate Change AI). It provides a hands-on, interactive framework for using the **En-ROADS Climate Solutions Simulator** to design global policy scenarios that can limit global warming to well below 2°C (and ideally 1.5°C) in accordance with the Paris Agreement.

Here is an in-depth breakdown of everything covered in the tutorial, structured by its core themes and system dynamics principles:

---

### 🎯 **1. Primary Objective & The En-ROADS Model**
* **Global Scope:** En-ROADS is a global system dynamics model where policy sliders simulate global-scale actions across energy, land use, consumption, agriculture, and carbon pricing.
* **Balanced Scenario Building:** The goal is not just to lower temperature, but to construct a realistic vision that balances **economic feasibility**, **social equity** (protecting vulnerable populations), and **environmental co-benefits** (such as reduced air pollution and biodiversity protection).

---

### 📉 **2. The Baseline Scenario & The Kaya Identity**
* **Default Trajectory:** Without new climate policies or intervention, the baseline scenario projects global warming to reach **~3.3°C by 2100**.
* **Kaya Identity Equation:** The tutorial explains emissions through the four Kaya drivers:
  \\[\text{CO}_2 \text{ Emissions from Energy} = \text{Population} \times \text{GDP per Capita} \times \text{Energy Intensity of GDP} \times \text{Carbon Intensity of Energy}\\]
* **Key Finding:** While **energy efficiency** (Energy/GDP) and **decarbonization** (\\(\text{CO}_2\\)/Energy) are improving over time, their progress is currently **outpaced by global population and economic GDP growth**, driving total emissions upward.

---

### ⚙️ **3. Core System Dynamics Principles**

The tutorial walks users through six key system dynamics concepts that govern how policies perform in the real world:

1. **Capital Stock Turnover Delay:**
   * Infrastructure (power plants, factories, buildings) has long operational lifespans (10–50+ years).
   * *Takeaway:* Developing a new zero-carbon energy source takes decades to scale up. To reach climate targets, we must simultaneously scale clean energy R&D *and* aggressively decarbonize existing infrastructure in the next 10–20 years.
2. **Price and Demand Feedback Loops:**
   * A **Carbon Price** raises the cost of energy proportionally to its carbon content.
   * *Takeaway:* It impacts coal hardest (most carbon-intensive), natural gas moderately, and oil slightly, while encouraging efficiency and driving down overall energy demand.
3. **Complementary Policies (Electrification):**
   * Renewable energy and nuclear power produce energy as **electricity**.
   * *Takeaway:* Subsidizing renewables alone has a limited impact unless paired with **electrification** of end-use sectors (e.g., electric vehicles in transport, heat pumps in buildings).
4. **Economies of Scale & Progress Ratios:**
   * Every time cumulative installed capacity of renewables doubles, costs drop by roughly **20%** (learning curve). Coal, by contrast, drops by only ~2% per doubling because its technology is already mature.
5. **Competition Between Energy Sources ("Crowding Out" & "Squeezing the Balloon"):**
   * **Crowding Out:** Subsidizing one clean energy source (e.g., new zero-carbon energy) can crowd out another clean source (like renewables) rather than purely replacing fossil fuels.
   * **Squeezing the Balloon:** Penalizing only one fossil fuel (e.g., taxing coal) often causes energy demand to shift into another fossil fuel (e.g., natural gas).
   * *Takeaway:* Comprehensive policies (like a unified carbon price) that raise the cost of *all* fossil fuels simultaneously are necessary to avoid simple demand shifting.
6. **Economic Damage Feedback:**
   * Climate damage (disasters, crop failures) acts as a **balancing feedback loop** by reducing economic growth and energy consumption, which slightly lowers future emissions—though at immense societal cost.

---

### 🌲 **4. Land Use, Forests & Non-\\(\text{CO}_2\\) Greenhouse Gases**
* **Forest Conservation:** Mature forests store vast amounts of carbon. Protecting existing forests avoids immediate carbon debt and maintains active natural carbon sinks.
* **Food & Agriculture:** Shifting diets toward plant-based foods and reducing food waste relieves pressure on land and cuts agricultural emissions.
* **Non-\\(\text{CO}_2\\) Gases (\\(\text{CH}_4, \text{N}_2\text{O}\\), F-Gases):** Non-\\(\text{CO}_2\\) emissions make up roughly **25% of current warming**. Because gases like methane and fluorinated refrigerants have extremely high Global Warming Potential (GWP), tackling waste, manure management, and gas leaks offers high-leverage temperature reductions.

---

### 🤖 **5. AI & Data Centers in En-ROADS**
* **Energy Demand Tracking:** En-ROADS explicitly models the growing energy demand and emissions from data centers and AI workloads.
* **Current Model Scope:** The model simulates AI's energy footprint, while proxy sliders are used to simulate AI's potential positive contributions to climate solution deployment or energy efficiency.
