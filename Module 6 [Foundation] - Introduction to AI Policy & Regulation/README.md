This module—**Module 6: Introduction to AI Policy & Regulation** (from Carnegie Mellon University's Civil and Environmental Engineering program)—examines how artificial intelligence intersects with policy, governance, risk management, public infrastructure, and climate outcomes.

Below is an in-depth explanation of the core topics, analytical frameworks, and regulatory landscapes covered in this module:

---

### **1. AI and Climate Risk Dynamics**
* **Amplifying Deep Uncertainties:** AI deployment interacts with both physical climate hazards and transition risks across three levels of uncertainty:
  * **Data Uncertainties:** Determining what climate extremes infrastructure must be engineered for and projecting future energy storage cost curves.
  * **Technology Uncertainties:** Assessing how AI and vehicle automation will alter net energy demand and greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions.
  * **Socio-Technical Uncertainties:** Evaluating economic impacts, equity, affordability, and deciding who pays for or benefits from technology adoption.
* **Climate Risk Framework:** Climate risk is explicitly modeled as a function of three variables:
  \\[\text{Climate Risk} = f(\text{Hazard}, \text{Exposure}, \text{Vulnerability})\\]
  * **Hazard:** The probability of a physical climate event occurring.
  * **Exposure:** The density of human population, property, and infrastructure assets in the hazard zone.
  * **Vulnerability:** The susceptibility to harm and economic value of exposed assets.

---

### **2. Risks of Generative AI & Systemic Vulnerabilities**
Drawing from the **NIST (2024)** risk classification, the module outlines major societal and technical risks posed by Generative AI:
* **Environmental Impacts:** High energy usage, carbon footprints from model training/inference, and cooling water consumption.
* **Information Integrity:** Confabulation/hallucinations, deepfakes, violent/hateful content, and automated dissemination of dangerous information (e.g., WMD instructions).
* **Governance & Trust:** Algorithmic bias, data privacy violations, intellectual property (IP) infringement, security exploits, and value chain integrity failures.

---

### **3. How AI Policy Shapes Climate Outcomes (The Kaack et al. Framework)**
The module incorporates the **Kaack et al. (2022)** multi-tier taxonomy to explain how AI affects global emissions across three distinct levels:
1. **System-Level Impacts (Macro):** Rebound effects, structural economic shifts, consumer behavioral changes, and long-term technological path dependency/lock-in.
2. **Immediate Application Impacts (Meso):** Optimizing or substituting operations across power systems, building energy efficiency, transportation networks, heavy industry, agriculture, and land use.
3. **Computing-Related Impacts (Micro):** Direct operational and embodied lifecycle emissions from manufacturing hardware (GPUs/CPUs, rare earth elements), data center computing, cooling, and internet physical infrastructure.

---

### **4. AI Policy as Climate & Energy Infrastructure Policy**
A central theme of the module is that **AI policy cannot be separated from energy and infrastructure policy**:
* **Surging Electricity Demand:** Projections from EPRI, LBNL, and IEA show exponential growth in U.S. data center electricity consumption through 2030.
* **Local Environmental Impacts:** Supplying data centers via fossil-fired power generation increases local air pollution and places stress on local water supplies.
* **Eroding Public Social License:** Polls indicate that **61% of Americans support a temporary pause or moratorium** on new data center construction nationwide due to grid and environmental concerns.
* **Grid Expansion Mandates:** Meeting climate targets alongside AI demand requires **doubling the electrical grid**, treating power grids as shared foundational clean infrastructure.

---

### **5. Global Approaches to AI Governance**
The module contrasts several overlapping international governance frameworks:
* **Risk-Based Governance (EU AI Act):** The world's first comprehensive legal framework for AI, categorizing systems into risk tiers ranging from *unacceptable risk* (banned) to *minimal risk*.
* **Rights-Based Governance:** Frameworks emphasizing human rights, democracy, and ethical standards, such as the *Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI* and *UNESCO Ethics of AI Recommendations*.
* **Standards-Based & Industry-Facing Governance:** Voluntary frameworks and technical standards, exemplified by the U.S. **NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0)** and its *Generative AI Profile* (focusing on Map, Measure, Manage, and Govern functions), alongside approaches in the U.K., Singapore, and China.
* **OECD Core AI Principles:** Global principles adopted by major economies emphasizing inclusive growth, fairness, transparency/explainability, safety, and accountability.

---

### **6. U.S. Federal & Subnational Policy Landscape**
* **White House Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights (2022):** Establishes 5 core protections: *Safe and Effective Systems*, *Algorithmic Discrimination Protections*, *Data Privacy*, *Notice and Explanation*, and *Human Alternatives/Fallback*.
* **Federal Executive Actions & OMB Memoranda:** Includes executive action plans for American AI leadership, Executive Order 14318 on accelerating data center permitting, and OMB Memoranda M-25-21 and M-25-22 governing federal AI deployment, risk management, and procurement.
* **Subnational State Legislation:** Rapid growth in state-level regulations targeting frontier-model safety, incident reporting, and algorithmic transparency (e.g., California's Transparency in Frontier AI Act, New York's RAISE Act, and Illinois' AI Safety Measures Act).

---

### **7. Core Principles for Clean Energy & Climate AI Policy**
The module concludes by establishing policy principles for aligning AI expansion with climate goals:
1. Ensures electricity remains affordable for consumers.
2. Requires AI deployments to add **more clean power to the grid than they consume**.
3. Enhances grid reliability, flexibility, and climate resilience against extreme weather events (heatwaves, storms, droughts).
4. Mandates open operational data and rising energy efficiency targets.
5. Aligns AI expansion with local and national environmental objectives.

## Key takeaways from the lecture:

- AI policy is not only about regulating algorithms, it also shapes infrastructure, investment, procurement and public services.

- AI risk can be viewed like climate risk: harm depends on probability, consequences, exposure and vulnerability.

- The hardest uncertainties are social: who benefits, who pays and how AI affects affordability and inequality.

- Environmental impacts are a core AI risk, and AI climate impact goes beyond its electricity use.

- No single governance model is enough: rights-, risk-, standards- and sector-based approaches must work together.

- AI is physically grounded: every digital system depends on energy, data centres, supply chains and climate-resilient infrastructure.

- Governance is shared: governments, firms, standards bodies, civil society and users all influence AI outcomes.

- Ultimately, AI policy is also climate, energy and infrastructure policy.
