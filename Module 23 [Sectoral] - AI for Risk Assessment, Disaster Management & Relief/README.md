### Key takeaways from today's lecture:

Mersedeh Tariverdi organized the session around one equation, Risk = Hazard x Exposure x Vulnerability. Climate change can make hazards more frequent or intense, but whether they become disasters depends heavily on how we prepare and respond. Forecasting and hazard mapping are important, but real risk reduction also comes from better warnings, resilient infrastructure, preparedness, and recovery resources which this equation encompasses.





- AI and ML can make forecasting and hazard mapping faster, cheaper, and more frequently updated. Combining AI with physics-based models can be particularly powerful. But models can struggle with rare extremes, new conditions, and situations where the underlying physics is poorly represented. This makes uncertainty, expert validation, and human oversight essential, especially when forecasts trigger costly decisions.



- The biggest gap is understanding who and what is actually exposed.Exposure is constantly changing. Satellite and computer vision models can map buildings, construction types, population, urban growth, displacement, and informal settlements, while mobile and census data can show how populations move throughout the day. Static census-based assessments can quickly become outdated. Vulnerability also needs to be considered alongside exposure, because the most vulnerable communities are often the least visible in conventional datasets. Accuracy and equity therefore need to be treated as separate objectives.



- The goal is to get better decisions, not just better models. Risk assessment brings hazard, exposure, and vulnerability together to estimate losses, casualties, displacement, and disruption. But uncertainty and data bias compound across these layers, particularly in places with limited data. The most useful approach is therefore to think across the before, during, and after disaster cycle from forecast-based financing and early action, to real-time situational awareness, to post-event damage assessment. Ultimately, the best use cases are not necessarily the most technically sophisticated ones; they are the ones that have good data, support a clear decision, fit institutional capacity, and meaningfully reduce risk for vulnerable communities.
